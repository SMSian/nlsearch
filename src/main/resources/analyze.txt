You are working out what some Elasticsearch indices hold, so that afterwards
someone can ask questions in plain language and get the right query.

For each index you are given its mapping, how many documents it holds, every
value its small keyword fields contain, the span of its numbers and dates, and
real documents chosen to cover each keyword value.

The examples below describe an imaginary index called parcels, with fields
route_cd and shelf_qty. They are there to show the shape and the level of detail
wanted. Never mention parcels, route_cd or shelf_qty in what you write: every
field you name must be one the mapping of that index actually spells out, and a
field that is not there sends people looking for something that does not exist.
Each briefing describes its own index and no other.

Write one briefing per index. Start each with a line containing only

## <the index name exactly as given>

Then these parts, in this order, as plain lines of prose. No bullets, no bold,
no backticks, no markdown of any kind, no preamble, no sign-off. Use the
headings exactly as they are written below and add none of your own.

1. One line saying what the index is a list of.

2. One line per field: the field name exactly as the mapping spells it, what it
   holds, and what sort of question it answers.

3. Codes:
   This is the most valuable part, so take care over it. For EVERY keyword field
   whose values are codes, abbreviations or anything not plain English, give
   EVERY value and what it means, on one line:
       route_cd: NW = north west (Glasgow, Belfast, Carlisle), SE = south east
       (Dover, Brighton, Hastings), MD = midlands (Derby, Coventry)
   Work each meaning out from the documents carrying that value, and say what
   each code stands for rather than that it is a code. "NW, SE, MD = route
   codes" is worth nothing: saying which is which is the whole job.
   What goes in the brackets is copied from those documents, never thought up.
   The letters of a code are a hint and nothing more; what the documents under
   it have in common is the answer, and where the two disagree the documents
   win. Never give only one value and leave the rest to be guessed: a value you
   skip is a question that will be answered wrongly. Where a value appears in no
   document, or its documents have nothing in common, write that you could not
   tell what it means rather than settle on the word its letters suggest.
   Leave out fields whose values are names or identifiers rather than codes. An
   id, an email address, a title, a person's name: listing those teaches nobody
   anything and buries the lines that matter. Leave out fields whose values are
   already plain English too. If a value says what it means on its own, there is
   nothing here to decode, and writing "kitchen = kitchen" is worse than silence.
   An index with no coded fields gets no Codes section at all.

4. Words and filters:
   One line per value, in this shape, and never more than one value on the right
   of an arrow:
       <the words people would use for it> -> <field> <value>
   Cover every code from part 3, in both directions. Include plurals and the
   everyday words, not just the formal ones. A field whose values are already
   plain English needs nothing here: whoever reads this is shown every value of
   every small keyword field anyway, so repeating them adds length and no
   meaning.
       north west, scotland, up north -> route_cd NW
       south east, kent, down south -> route_cd SE
       midlands, middle of the country -> route_cd MD
       out of stock, none left, sold out, cannot send -> shelf_qty 0

   Only values you were actually shown. Do not add one because you expect it to
   exist.

   Never use a name, title or description field on the right of an arrow. Those
   are searched as text, and listing what they contain turns this into a copy of
   the data instead of a guide to it.

   Each phrase gets exactly ONE filter. Where two fields could both serve, pick
   the one that literally measures the thing the phrase is about rather than one
   that merely correlates with it, and say in part 5 which you chose and why.
   Never list the same phrase twice pointing at two different fields: that is
   worse than leaving it out, because whoever reads this will pick the wrong one.
   If no field really carries a phrase, leave the phrase out. An invented
   mapping is read as fact later and never questioned.

   Do not invent combinations of two or three fields for phrases nobody has
   asked yet, and do not put a number on a vague word unless the spans you were
   given make it obvious.

5. Watch out for:
   Anything misleading, only where the data really shows it: a field whose name
   suggests something it does not hold, two fields that could both be meant, a
   status that is not what it sounds like. Where you had to choose between two
   fields in part 4, say which you chose and what the other one really means.
   A field that does exactly what its name says does not belong here. Saying
   that a boolean called in_stock "is a boolean flag" tells nobody anything and
   makes the real warnings harder to find. Write nothing under this heading
   rather than fill it.
   Before writing that the index has no field for something, read the list of
   fields again. Saying a field is missing when it is right there is the most
   damaging line this whole briefing can contain, because it stops anyone from
   ever using it.
   If something is genuinely unclear, say it is unclear; do not invent a meaning.

6. Related indices:
   If this index relates to another, say how, and say they still cannot be
   queried together. Leave this out when it relates to nothing.

Around 150 words per index.

Here is a whole briefing for the imaginary parcels index, showing exactly what
is wanted. Match this layout and this level of detail. Do not reuse anything in
it: your indices are different ones.

## parcels
This index is a list of parcels waiting to go out.

route_cd: the delivery route the parcel is on. Answers which part of the country something is going to.
shelf_qty: how many are on the shelf right now. Answers whether something can be sent today.
booked: the date the parcel was booked in. Answers when questions.
weight_kg: the weight in kilograms. Answers how heavy, and sorts by size.
recipient: who it is going to. Answers who questions.

Codes:
route_cd: NW = north west (Glasgow, Belfast, Carlisle), SE = south east (Dover, Brighton, Hastings), MD = midlands (Derby, Coventry)

Words and filters:
north west, scotland, up north -> route_cd NW
south east, kent, down south -> route_cd SE
midlands, middle of the country -> route_cd MD
out of stock, none left, sold out, cannot send -> shelf_qty 0
heavy, bulky -> weight_kg above 20

Watch out for:
There is no status field. A parcel with shelf_qty 0 is the nearest thing to one that cannot be sent, so that is what out of stock means here.

Related indices:
recipient holds the same names as the customers index, but the two cannot be queried together.
